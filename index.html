Reversal Risk Scoring for Guaranteed ACH Payments

A lender pays a vendor to insure every bill payment against reversing — but most payments were never going to reverse. This case study tests whether the lender's own data can spot the safe ones well enough to stop paying for them. The answer: nearly, but not yet — and getting there meant finding and fixing a serious bug in the model's first, too-good-to-be-true result.

01

The problem

An ACH bill payment can look successful and then reverse days later — the money comes back out, and the lender is on the hook. A third-party vendor sells insurance against exactly this: for a percentage of every payment routed through its "guaranteed" product, the vendor eats the loss if that payment reverses.

Roughly 96% of payments were never going to reverse. That means most of what the lender pays the vendor is insuring transactions that were already safe. If a model can reliably tell the safe payments apart from the risky ones, the safe ones can skip the vendor and its fee.

That's a buy-vs-build question, not a modeling question — the model is just the instrument for answering it.

02

The data

82,841 bill payments over two months: 42,138 for training the model, 40,703 held out to test it. Of those, 3,385 reversed — a 4.1% rate.

In plain terms

If a model just guessed "safe" on every single payment, it would be right 95.9% of the time — and completely useless, since it would catch none of the risky ones. That's why plain accuracy never appears on this page, and why the model is weighted to take the rare, risky cases seriously.

What the model sees

Two feature families. Twenty-one signals purchased from the vendor:

  • Return-risk scores
  • Account balance measures and percentiles
  • Debit and credit transaction counts, 10/30/60/90-day windows
  • Overdraft event counts

Five more the lender already owned and wasn't using:

  • Prior reversal count
  • Prior reversal total
  • Prior transaction count
  • Prior reversal rate
  • Days since last reversal

About 45% of rows are missing vendor signals entirely — those payments ran through a different processor the vendor never scored.

The split is by month, not random: train on the earlier month, test on the later one. A random split would let the model learn from a later month to predict an earlier one — not something it can do once it's actually running. Splitting by time mirrors how it operates in production.

04

The bug

The first version of the model scored 0.97 AUC — near-perfect. That should have been the tip-off.

One feature counted how many times a customer had reversed a payment before. But the way it was written, a payment that was itself a reversal counted itself. Every risky payment in the training data arrived carrying a clue that it was risky. The model wasn't predicting anything — it was reading the answer key.

How it surfaced: not from review. It came up while building a different feature — days since the customer's last reversal — which forced the question of what "last" means relative to the payment being scored. That question exposed that the original feature had never asked it.

The fix: count only payments that came strictly before the one being scored. That count also has to look back across the customer's entire history, not just the study period — otherwise customers who joined early look artificially clean, since their real prior reversals would fall outside a truncated window.

// counts only rows strictly before the one being scored prior_reversal_count(customer, t) = count of reversals by this customer where reversal_time < t — computed over the customer's full history first, study-window restriction applied after

This was not a train/test split problem. The split was correct the whole time. The bug lived inside the feature definition, which means no amount of validation discipline would have caught it — cross-validation would have reported the same inflated number, just as confidently.

05

Testing it properly

The corrected model was scored on a later month it had never seen — a real forward test, not a re-shuffle of the same data. 70,842 payments, 3,212 of which reversed.

AUC 0.8462, average precision 0.3626.

Average precision barely moved — 0.366 in testing, 0.363 scored forward on brand-new data. That's the meaningful part: the model holds up.

The riskiest 10% of payments contains 52% of all the reversals.

Best case clears about 31% of payments (30.9%) while still catching 98% of reversals (98.4%) — real, but the margin is thin, and it turns negative above about 0.10. Precision at the best setting is 6.5%: of every 100 payments flagged risky, about 7 actually reverse. The model is a strong ranker (it puts payments in the right order) and a weak classifier (pick any single cutoff and most of what it flags turns out fine) — and ranking is what this use case needs, but not by enough yet to justify dropping the vendor.

A qualified negative is still a useful answer. It tells the business exactly how much better the model needs to get, and which customers to focus on first.

06

What the data suggested, and what happened when I acted on it

Two of these were found, acted on, and measured. One is still open.

Finding 1 · Acted on

Recency was missing, so I added it.

Days since last reversalCustomersActually reversed
0–302,50735.2%

365+5,6134.5%

An 8x spread in real outcomes — 35.2% down to 4.5% — that the model was compressing into about 2x, because it had no sense of time. After adding it as a feature, average precision went 0.319 → 0.366, and it became the single most important feature in the model.

Finding 2 · Acted on

One prior reversal means nothing without a denominator.

Two reversals out of three payments is a very different customer from two out of two hundred, and the model couldn't tell them apart. Adding prior transaction count and prior reversal rate fixed the worst calibration error: the model used to overestimate risk about 7x for customers with no history, and now it's accurate there.

Finding 3 · Still open

Cold start is still open.

78.7% of payments come from customers with no reversal history at all, and 40% of all reversals come from that group. The model's best features are blind to them.

Proposal

A separate scoring path using account age, first-payment behavior, and signup-time signals. This one is unsolved.

08

What I'd do differently

  1. 01Check feature definitions for time correctness before reporting anything. The split gets checked by habit; the feature definition doesn't.
  2. 02Treat any single feature carrying most of the model's weight as a red flag to investigate, not a result to present.
  3. 03Lead with average precision on rare-event problems. AUC hid how weak the classifier really was.
  4. 04Build the calibration check before the threshold sweep, not after.

Figures on this page are exact, drawn from a real internal project at a US fintech lender. Company name, vendor identity, dollar amounts, and internal field names have been withheld; everything else — every rate, count, and score — is reported as measured.
